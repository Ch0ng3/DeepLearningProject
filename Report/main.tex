\begin{abstract}
Image classifiers trained on photographs often fail on paintings, cartoons and sketches of the same objects. We study this domain generalization problem on PACS with the DomainBed benchmark. We first reproduce the ERM baseline with a supervised ResNet-50 (84.9\% average accuracy on unseen domains, versus 85.5\% reported in the paper). We then replace the backbone with a self-supervised DINOv2 ViT-B/14 and a language-supervised CLIP ViT-B/16 and compare three adaptation methods: a linear classifier on frozen features, LoRA and full fine-tuning. A linear probe alone already raises the average accuracy to 94.5\% (DINOv2) and 96.7\% (CLIP); CLIP's advantage comes from the most abstract domains, cartoon and sketch, but its linear probe is strongly under-confident. Training the backbone helps only when it is done conservatively: LoRA on CLIP gives the best result (97.1\%), on par with CLIP zero-shot without any training (97.2\%), and fixes its calibration, whereas full fine-tuning lowers the accuracy of both backbones (93.5\% and 95.4\%), mostly on sketch, where the selected checkpoint becomes unstable and over-confident. \todo{Milestone~3 research question and answer.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A classifier is usually trained and tested on images from the same distribution. Domain generalization asks for more: a model trained on some visual domains must work on a domain it has never seen. PACS~\cite{li2017pacs} is a standard benchmark for this problem: 9{,}991 images of 7 classes (dog, elephant, giraffe, guitar, horse, house, person) in 4 very different styles (photo, art painting, cartoon, sketch). The usual protocol is leave-one-domain-out: train on three domains, test on the fourth.

DomainBed~\cite{gulrajani2021domainbed} showed that, under a fair model-selection protocol, plain Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM) is hard to beat. Its backbone, however, is an ImageNet-supervised ResNet-50. Recent foundation models are pretrained differently: DINOv2~\cite{oquab2023dinov2} with self-supervision on curated images, CLIP~\cite{radford2021clip} with natural-language supervision on web-scale image--text pairs. How these pretraining strategies, and the way the backbone is adapted, affect generalization to unseen styles is the subject of this project.

Our contributions follow the three milestones of the project:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Reproduction.} We reproduce DomainBed's ERM result on PACS within 0.6 points on average (Section~\ref{sec:m1}).
  \item \textbf{Improvement.} We replace the backbone with DINOv2 and CLIP and compare linear probing, LoRA~\cite{hu2022lora} and full fine-tuning under the same protocol and data split, changing one factor at a time and reporting accuracy, cost and calibration (Section~\ref{sec:m2}).

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Domain generalization.} DomainBed~\cite{gulrajani2021domainbed} re-implemented many algorithms under one protocol and found that, with model selection restricted to training-domain data, ERM matches most specialized methods. We adopt its protocol and its code.

\textbf{Pretrained backbones.} DINOv2~\cite{oquab2023dinov2} learns general-purpose features by self-distillation without labels; its frozen features give strong linear-probe results. CLIP~\cite{radford2021clip} aligns images and captions with a contrastive loss and enables zero-shot classification; it is known to be robust to natural distribution shifts.

\textbf{Adaptation.} A linear probe trains only a classifier on frozen features. LoRA~\cite{hu2022lora} adds trainable low-rank updates to frozen weight matrices. Full fine-tuning updates every weight but can distort pretrained features and reduce robustness out of distribution, which WiSE-FT~\cite{wortsman2022wiseft} counters by interpolating zero-shot and fine-tuned weights.

\section{Experimental Protocol}\label{sec:protocol}
All experiments use the official DomainBed code with ERM on PACS. Each domain is split into an \emph{in} part (80\%) and an \emph{out} part (20\%). The model is trained on the \emph{in} parts of three domains, a checkpoint is evaluated every 300 steps, and the checkpoint with the highest mean accuracy on the \emph{out} parts of the training domains is selected (\emph{training-domain validation}). We report the accuracy of the selected checkpoint on the held-out domain. The held-out domain is never used for selection or tuning; DomainBed's oracle selection is reported only for reference. Every run uses data split and seed~0, identical to Milestone~1, so all methods are compared on the same data. Training uses 32 images per training domain per step, DomainBed's data augmentation, $224\times224$ inputs and Adam. Besides accuracy, we log for the held-out domain the confusion matrix and the expected calibration error (ECE, 15 bins) of the selected checkpoint. Experiments run on a single NVIDIA A100 in Google Colab.

\section{Milestone 1: Reproduction}\label{sec:m1}
We run DomainBed's ERM with the torchvision ImageNet ResNet-50 (the default \texttt{resnet50\_augmix} backbone is switched off to match the paper), default hyperparameters (learning rate $5\times10^{-5}$, no weight decay) and 5000 steps, once per held-out domain. PACS was taken from the Hugging Face copy because DomainBed's download failed.

\section{Milestone 2: Improving the Backbone}\label{sec:m2}
\subsection{Backbones and adaptation methods}
We change one factor at a time: the backbone (ResNet-50 $\to$ DINOv2 ViT-B/14 $\to$ CLIP ViT-B/16) or the adaptation method (linear probe $\to$ LoRA $\to$ full fine-tuning), keeping data, split, protocol and training code fixed. Every run uses the data split of Milestone~1, and every experiment, including discarded early versions, is recorded in the experiment log of the repository. The learning rate and the number of steps are part of each adaptation method (standard values, no search). All vision-transformer runs use fewer training steps than the ResNet-50 baseline (1500--2000 vs.\ 5000) and take 5--8 minutes each on an A100, so their gains do not come from a larger training budget; the measured cost of each method is reported in Table~\ref{tab:m2}.

As a reference that needs no training, we also evaluate \textbf{CLIP zero-shot}: the classifier is the normalized text embedding of the prompt ``a photo of a \{class\}.'' for each class. It has no hyperparameters and no model selection, and it is evaluated on the whole held-out domain (the trained models on its 80\% \emph{in} part).

\textbf{DINOv2 ViT-B/14} (LVD-142M) is loaded from the official repository; following DomainBed and the DINOv2 linear-evaluation recipe, the feature is the concatenation of the class tokens of the last four blocks and the mean patch token ($5\times768=3840$ dimensions). \textbf{CLIP ViT-B/16} (OpenCLIP, LAION-2B) uses only the image tower; its 512-dimensional embedding is the feature, and inputs are re-normalized from ImageNet to CLIP statistics.

The \textbf{linear probe} freezes the backbone and trains a linear classifier (learning rate $5\times10^{-5}$, 2000 steps). \textbf{LoRA} freezes the backbone and adds rank-8 adapters ($\alpha=16$) to the fused query--key--value and output projections of every attention block, 442{,}368 parameters on both backbones (on CLIP through a parametrization of its attention weights), trained with the classifier (learning rate $5\times10^{-4}$, 1500 steps). \textbf{Full fine-tuning} trains all weights (learning rate $10^{-5}$ for the backbone and $10^{-4}$ for the new classifier, 1500 steps). Learning rates differ because the methods train very different numbers of parameters; the cost of each method is measured and reported in Table~\ref{tab:m2}.

\subsection{Results}
\textbf{Backbone.} Replacing the ResNet-50 by a frozen foundation model with only a linear classifier on top already gives the largest improvement of the project: +9.6 points with DINOv2 and +11.8 with CLIP (Table~\ref{tab:m2}, Fig.~\ref{fig:domains}). The gains concentrate on the domains furthest from photographs: on sketch, accuracy rises from 78.3 to 87.7 (DINOv2) and 92.1 (CLIP); on photo, where ResNet-50 is already at 97.8, all models are close to the ceiling.

\textbf{DINOv2 vs.\ CLIP.} CLIP beats DINOv2 by 2.2 points on average even though its feature has 7.5$\times$ fewer dimensions. The difference comes entirely from cartoon (+6.1) and sketch (+4.4), whereas DINOv2 is slightly better on art (+1.0) and photo (+0.5). A plausible explanation is the supervision signal: language descriptions group objects by semantic category regardless of rendering style, while self-supervised features also encode appearance statistics of natural images. With a single seed, differences below 1--2 points (art, photo) are not conclusive.

\textbf{Cost.} Both linear probes train only a few thousand parameters, take about 5 minutes per run and need about 1.3\,GB of GPU memory. LoRA on DINOv2 trains 469k parameters but must back-propagate through the whole backbone: it is 2.2$\times$ slower per step (0.240\,s), takes 7.8 minutes per run and needs 10.6\,GB; LoRA on CLIP (446k parameters) needs 8.9\,GB and 6.2 minutes. Full fine-tuning trains 86.6M (DINOv2) and 111.8M (CLIP) parameters and needs 17.1\,GB and 12.8\,GB, at 0.256 and 0.220\,s per step (8.0 and 6.9 minutes per run). DINOv2 is the more expensive backbone to train despite having fewer parameters, because its $14\times14$ patches give 256 tokens per image instead of 196. LoRA therefore saves 30--38\% of the memory of full fine-tuning but only 6--14\% of the time, since the backward pass still traverses the whole network.

\textbf{Adaptation method.} On DINOv2, LoRA reaches the same average as the linear probe (94.5) but redistributes the accuracy: it gains on cartoon (+3.2) and loses on art (\textminus1.6) and sketch (\textminus1.3). Adapting the attention layers fits the training domains almost perfectly (99--100\% on their validation splits) and the best checkpoint is reached early (300--1200 of 1500 steps), so the additional capacity mainly specializes the features to the source styles instead of improving the most distant domain. On CLIP, LoRA gives the best result of the project, 97.1 (+0.4 over the linear probe: art +1.7, cartoon \textminus0.6, photo and sketch +0.3), a difference within single-seed noise.

Full fine-tuning is the worst adaptation on both backbones: 93.5 on DINOv2 (\textminus1.0 vs.\ the linear probe) and 95.4 on CLIP (\textminus1.3 vs.\ the linear probe, \textminus1.7 vs.\ LoRA). The loss is concentrated on sketch, the domain furthest from the training styles: \textminus2.9 on DINOv2 and \textminus2.5 on CLIP. The training curves show why model selection cannot prevent it. On the sketch runs, the validation accuracy of the training domains stays between 97.5\% and 99.6\% at every checkpoint, while the sketch accuracy swings between 74.4\% and 84.8\% (DINOv2) and between 79.0\% and 91.0\% (CLIP); likewise, CLIP's art accuracy drops from 96.9\% to 90.1\% at the last step while validation changes by less than 3 points. Updating all weights thus distorts the pretrained features in a way that is invisible on the source domains, which is the failure mode that WiSE-FT~\cite{wortsman2022wiseft} is designed to fix and that Milestone~3 examines.

\textbf{Trained vs.\ zero-shot CLIP.} Without any training, CLIP zero-shot reaches 97.2, on par with the best trained model (LoRA, 97.1) and above the linear probe (96.7) and full fine-tuning (95.4). It is the best model on art (98.8), cartoon (99.1) and photo (99.8); training on the three source domains helps only on sketch, and only slightly (92.4 with LoRA vs.\ 91.0). Full fine-tuning is below zero-shot on all four domains, by 0.8 to 2.8 points. A prompt ensemble that also describes the styles (``a painting / cartoon / sketch of a \{class\}'') reaches 97.6, but it uses knowledge of the test styles, so we report it only as a reference. On PACS, therefore, CLIP already contains most of the robustness to style changes, and fine-tuning on a few source domains mainly risks losing part of it.

\subsection{Error analysis and calibration}
The confusion matrices of the held-out domains show that most remaining errors are between four-legged animals in the abstract domains. On sketch, DINOv2 labels 143 giraffes and 60 dogs as horses and 69 guitars as persons; CLIP makes the same giraffe$\to$horse (82) and dog$\to$horse (53) errors, but fewer. On cartoon, DINOv2 recognizes only 74\% of the horses (41 predicted as dog, 24 as giraffe), which explains most of its 6-point gap to CLIP on that domain. On art, CLIP's main error is the class person, spread over dog, guitar, horse and house.

The two backbones differ sharply in calibration. DINOv2's linear probe is well calibrated (ECE 2.0\%), whereas CLIP's is strongly \emph{under}-confident: its mean confidence is 68--82\% while its accuracy is 92--99\%, giving an ECE of 21\%. The most likely cause is that, with the same classifier learning rate, the small-norm CLIP embedding produces small logits, so the softmax stays flat even when the prediction is correct. Accuracy alone therefore hides that CLIP's confidence scores are not reliable on the unseen domains, a point that Milestone~3 investigates further. Adapting the backbone changes this picture: with LoRA, DINOv2 becomes over-confident on sketch (mean confidence 95\% for 86\% accuracy, ECE 9.0\% against 2.5\% for the linear probe) and collapses many animals onto the class horse (160 dogs and 142 giraffes). The same happens with full fine-tuning: on sketch, DINOv2 labels 161 giraffes and 153 dogs as horses (only 61\% of the dogs are correct) with a mean confidence of 93\% for 85\% accuracy (ECE 8.4\%), and CLIP 107 giraffes and 71 dogs (ECE 6.2\%). On CLIP, by contrast, both LoRA and full fine-tuning remove the under-confidence of the linear probe (ECE 1.8\% and 2.5\%): once the backbone is trained, the logits grow and the confidence follows the accuracy on art, cartoon and photo (ECE 0.5--1.7\%). LoRA on CLIP also spreads its sketch errors evenly instead of collapsing onto horse (45 horses as dogs, 44 giraffes as horses, 43 giraffes as dogs), but it is still over-confident there (97\% confidence for 92\% accuracy, ECE 5.0\%). In summary, every method that trains the backbone becomes over-confident on sketch, and only DINOv2's linear probe stays calibrated there (ECE 2.5\%).

\section{Limitations}
All Milestone~2 results come from a single seed and a single data split, so small differences are not significant; Milestone~3 uses three seeds. We did not search hyperparameters: the learning rates are standard values for each adaptation method, and the vision transformers are trained for fewer steps than the ResNet-50 (1500--2000 vs.\ 5000), which favors the cheaper methods. The linear probes often select one of the last checkpoints, so they may be slightly under-trained, which may also contribute to the small logits and under-confidence of CLIP's linear probe. CLIP zero-shot is evaluated on the whole held-out domain rather than on its 80\% \emph{in} part, which adds sampling noise of a few tenths of a point. Finally, PACS is small and has only seven classes; conclusions may not transfer to larger benchmarks such as DomainNet.
